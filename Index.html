Introduction & The Basic Idea

This is a tutorial for my method of solving Rubik's Cube. It is intended both for beginners and experienced cubers. There is no group theory or written notation involved, I just show you how to move.

Using this method, I won the 1981 Swedish championships, and finished 4th in the first world championships in Budapest 1982. I also won a minor world championship in 2005.

Why not the layer-by-layer method?

Most people solve the cube layer by layer. This is a simple way for the human mind to approach the problem, but it is useless for speed cubing. No matter how good you are, you will use more than 100 moves. Going for speed, I use 55–60 moves on average. Going for few moves, I average 40–45.

The fundamental problem with the layer method: When you have completed the first layer, you can do nothing without breaking it up. So you break it, do something useful, then restore it. Break it, do something, restore it. Again and again. In a good solution you do something useful all the time. The first layer is in the way of the solution, not a part of it!

Basic Cube Facts

You need to understand what the parts of the cube are. It is not 54 stickers. And it is not 27 little cubes. It is 12 edge and 8 corner pieces moving inside a frame of 6 unmovable center pieces. Each edge has two stickers, and each corner has three stickers.

Centers never move. No matter how much you turn, the 6 center pieces always remain in place. The "green layer" is always the one with the green center, regardless of how many green stickers are on it otherwise.

The common error by people who don't know how the cube is built is to try to move individual stickers, instead of pieces. They think they have accomplished something when they "complete one side". This is a quite useless position, since almost none of the pieces are in the right position. That they happen to have the right sticker colour by the center is just a pretty accident, and does not mean you're anywhere near a solution.

When reassembling a disassembled cube, put it back in the solved state. If not, you have a 93% (11/12) chance of getting it in an unsolvable position.

Terminology & Notation

As far as I know, there is no universally accepted terminology for cubing. For this tutorial we use the following exact conventions.

TermDefinition
CornerA physical plastic corner piece. Has 3 stickers.
EdgeA physical plastic edge piece. Has 2 stickers.
PieceA corner or an edge.
StickerA coloured sticker. Nine on each side.
SideOne of the 6 sides of the cube. A 2-dimensional concept.
LayerThe cube consists of three layers (top/middle/bottom). 3-dimensional. The "green layer" is the one with the green center, regardless of how many green stickers are on it.
Twist / twistedHow a piece is oriented. A corner can be twisted in 3 different ways, an edge in 2.
Turn / turningThe physical act of rotating a side.
Position (noun)A place in the cube, relative to the centers. Positions don't move; pieces do.
Move sequenceA useful sequence of turns. Named sequences in this tutorial: Niklas™, Sune™, AntiSune™, Allan™, Bert™, Arne™, Bruno™, Rune™, Benny™.

Standard Notation

Face letters: F (Front) · B (Back) · R (Right) · L (Left) · U (Up) · D (Down)

Suffixes: none = 90° clockwise  ·  ' (prime) = 90° counter-clockwise  ·  2 = 180°

Slice moves: M (between L and R, like L) · E (between U and D, like D) · S (between F and B, like F)

Examples: R = right face clockwise  ·  U' = top face counter-clockwise  ·  F2 = front face 180°

STEP 1 Build the 2×2×2 Corner

The goal in this step is to build a 2×2×2 block. Or more precisely, to place one corner and its three neighbouring edges in their correct positions, solving 4 out of the 20 cube pieces.

Beginner

For the beginner it is often easiest to pick a corner that you will always start with. This makes it easy to find the pieces you need. We will use the blue-yellow-orange corner. This means that the three edges are the blue-orange, the orange-yellow, and the blue-yellow.

What if there IS NO blue-yellow-orange corner? That's normal. There are many different colour schemes on cubes in the world, so whatever colour arrangement I pick for this site, it will not match most cubes. The method works for any set of colours, so try to focus on the method, not the particular colours used as examples.

There are many special cases, but this is the basic way I solve it:

  1. Pair up the corner with an edge.
  2. Pair up another edge with a centre.
  3. Join the pairs from 1 & 2 to make a 2×2×1 block.
  4. Join the remaining edge with the 2 remaining centres.
  5. Put it all together in the final move.

Intermediate

After you can easily do one corner, you should start looking at all 8 corners and choosing to start with the one that is fastest to solve. Normally, one or two are real easy (and some are hard). Picking the easy one makes quite a difference.

At competitions there is normally a 15-second period where you get to look at the cube before timing starts. This is what you do during that time (though 2–5 seconds are usually enough).

Advanced

Try to be aware of where the four pieces are at all times. You should be able to visualise the full step solution in your mind before starting. Spend a lot of time looking at this step, and you will start seeing 5 moves ahead or more surprisingly often.

STEP 2 Expand to 2×2×3

After step 1, we have a solved part of the cube, and we have three sides that we can move freely, without breaking what we have accomplished. Not bad!

In step 2 we expand what we have to a 2×2×3 block. That is, we add one corner and two edges to the solved block, going from 4 to 7 solved pieces of 20.

Beginner

Choose one of the three possible corners to expand to. This is quite similar to how you work in step 1. When you're familiar with step 1 you should have no problem. But be sure not to break up the 2×2×2 block! You need to be aware where it is at all times.

Intermediate

You have three corners to choose from. It's important to pick the easiest, just like in step 1. The hardest positions are when a piece is in its right position, but twisted the wrong way. The easiest way to deal with that is to just pick another corner to solve!

These simple and very useful moves took months to figure out — they involve breaking the holy 2×2×2 block. As an exercise, try to find how to do the example above in 7 moves instead of 9 by breaking the 2×2×2. Hint: First move: turn the green layer ¼ clockwise. Second move breaks the 2×2×2.

Sometimes, the first move of step 3 will be the opposite of the last move in this phase. So with some foresight you can skip both. Also, while doing the last turn of step 2, you can often stop half way, and fix a pair of bad edges midway.

STEP 3 Twist the Edges

The basic idea of the method is to solve the entire cube from here by just turning the 2 free sides. But if you try to do that, you soon discover that some edges are always twisted the wrong way. We call those the "bad" edges. We need to fix that before we move on. Step 3 is this fix.

Step 3 can seem incomprehensible before you "get" it, but it is really the simplest step in the method.

Beginner

Basically, we need to do 2 things in step 3.

1. Identify the bad edges. There are 7 edges not part of the 2×2×3. Pick any one of them, say the red/blue one. Using only the 2 free layers (that is, not breaking up the 2×2×3), place it between the red and blue centre pieces. If it's twisted right, it's good. Otherwise it's bad. Make a (mental or on paper) note of it, and check the next edge. When checking the second and later edges, do not try to keep the previous edges in place. It is not only extremely difficult, but also completely unnecessary. Once you know if an edge is good or bad, you know.

2. Turn bad edges into good edges. There are always an even number of bad edges. You can make them good in pairs. The simplest way is the 3-move sequence shown below. Just place two bad edges in the positions of the coloured edges, and do it.

Intermediate

Since we only want to know if an edge would be correctly twisted if it was placed in its correct location, we don't have to waste moves actually moving it there. It is easy to see which edges are bad without moving them.

Let's call the colours of the two remaining centres A and B. Ignore everything except the stickers on the (unsolved) edges with colour A or B. There are four simple rules:

RuleConditionStatus
1An edge with an A colour sticker next to the A colour centreGOOD
2An edge with an A colour sticker away from the A colour centreBAD

4An edge with a B colour sticker away from the A colour centreGOOD

Advanced

Why does the move sequence work? It's not just a "magic move". You can understand it. What happens is [1] one bad edge is flipped when moved to the other layer, [2] another bad edge replaces it, and is [3] (un)flipped when the first turn is reversed. Both edges get flipped in the process and so become good. Once you understand that, you can figure out several other moves that do the same thing.

STEP 4 Finish 2 Layers

Back to adding solved pieces! In step 4 we go from 7 to 12 solved pieces out of 20. This is clearly the hardest step. Not that it's all that hard — you could also say that the other steps are really easy!

Key constraint: You can only turn the two free sides. And halfway through, you can't even do many of those moves, since they would destroy what you already built. Keep a firm grip around the 2×2×3 with one hand at all times.

Step 4a — First corner and its two edges

What you do in step 4 is pretty much the same thing that you do in step 1 and 2. But you have only two sides you can turn. From the piece perspective, the goal of step 4 is to add 2 corners and 3 edges to the 2×2×3 block, to make it into a 2×3×3 block (2 full layers).

To do what you want, you first have to move pieces into a position where the desired turns can be made on the two free sides. Make sure to only turn the two unsolved sides!

Note that you now have two completed 2×2×3 blocks! Remember which of them you started with. If you accidentally start using the other, bad edges will reappear, and you have to go back to Step 3.

Step 4b — Final two pieces

Putting the final 2 pieces in place without breaking anything is almost claustrophobic. Since you can't break up the 2×2×1 block you made in 4a, you have no choices in how to move the last layer. You must wiggle it back and forth, ¼ turn each time. So your only degree of freedom is to choose one of three possible turns with the other layer every other turn!

The normal way of doing this is to join the two pieces in the upper layer, and then put the pair in place.

R U R' U' × 3

Intermediate

If you can place the final corner by itself, that is often good. There are easy ways to place the final edge, by very briefly breaking up our previous achievements. For the mirrored positions, almost identical sequences apply. Note that the second one is a variation of Allan™ from step 7!

STEP 5 Position the Corners

Now we're at the final layer. Here we do not think as much. We recognise patterns and apply rules.

Common mistake: If the edges are not twisted correctly here, you have either not done step 3 correctly, or screwed up the edges by breaking the 2×2×3 in step 4.

When starting this step, you should have only one layer remain unsolved, and the edges in it already correctly twisted ("forming a cross"). In this step we will place the top corners correctly, nothing else. The corners normally look pretty unsolved after completing step 5 — you have to inspect them to know they're right.

To do this we need a tool that moves the top corners without twisting the top edges. This tool is Niklas™.

Niklas™
R U' L' U R' U' L U
Swaps the two corners opposite the layer where the first move is made. 7-turn sequence. Named by Lars Petrus.
R U' L' U R' U' L U
Niklas™
L' U R U' L U R' U'
Niklas™ Mirror

Beginner — odds and procedure

  • 1/6 of the time, the corners will already be correct. Go to step 6.
  • 1/6 of the time, two corners diagonally opposed will have switched. Two Niklas™ moves needed.
  • 4/6 of the time, two adjacent corners will have switched. One Niklas™.

Turn the top layer until two corners are in correct positions. The two others are either correct or need swapping. Niklas™ will swap the two corners opposite the layer where you make its first move.

Intermediate

Find two adjacent corners with the same (non-top) colour, and move them to the side of that colour. If the corners are correct, all 4 will be in place. If adjacent corners are switched, two will be correct, and you can see which ones. If diagonal corners are switched, all four corners will be placed wrong.

Here is a useful move sequence that directly switches two corners diagonally:

R' F R' B2 R F' R' B2 R2
Direct diagonal swap (Eve)

Advanced

Note that Niklas™ twists the corners in the same way as Sune™ does, so the same "target" rules apply. A Niklas™ that hits a target starts with the same turn as a Sune™ that hits the same target. About half the time you can avoid one Sune™ in step 6 this way.

For the advanced cuber, steps 5–7 meld together to one single step of dozens or even hundreds of special cases. See the Full Step 5+6 Index.

STEP 6 Twist the Corners

So far we have the top edges twisted right, and the top corners placed right. The next step is twisting the corners right. This can be done using only one simple move sequence: Sune™. It twists three of the four top corners. It also moves top edges, but we don't care about that yet.

Step 6 can always be done with 2 Sunes or less. You do need to learn Sune™ to use this method.

The target of Sune™

The target of a Sune™ is the corner sticker on the opposite side from the first turn, on the side of the corner moving up. Look for where the black X's are in the animations in relation to the first move.

The four rules (assuming, say, yellow top layer)

  • Rule 0 — No correct corners: Target one of the four yellow stickers. After Sune™ there will be one correct corner. Use Rule 1.
  • Rule 1 — One correct corner: Target as shown. This solves the corners and completes step 6.
  • Rule 2 — Two correct corners: Target one of the two non-yellow stickers of a twisted corner. After Sune™ there will be one correct corner. Use Rule 1.
  • Rule 3 — Three correct corners: Impossible.
  • Rule 4 — Four correct corners: Already solved — go to Step 7.

Intermediate

Doing Sune™ backwards (AntiSune) twists corners the same way as Sune™, but moves the edges differently. Note the target! You can hit a target with either a Sune™ or an AntiSune, and often you can skip step 7 by choosing right.

Block Building Patterns

The core of this method is to build blocks. It is what steps 1, 2 and 4 are all about. This can be done intuitively, but with experience you will pick up some patterns you can immediately recognise, which is one of the keys to doing this fast.

"Simple join"

This is trivial in a way, but I don't want to overlook it since it is really the one I use the most. It's the second thing I start looking for on a mixed cube, if there are no already formed pairs. It simply consists of finding a corner and edge that are one move away from forming a pair.

"Double join"

Also very basic and very useful. It doesn't "just happen" as much as a simple join, but it's the most common second goal once you've formed a pair. Since we're building a 2×2×1 block a centre needs to be part of it, as well as the corner and 2 edges.

"Swing"

Brilliant when you can get it. That's pretty often for S1, and occasionally in S2. The first move is just a setup move for a double join in the second move. Both the lone edge and the pair can be in other positions.

A very good Step 1 start, and it sometimes happens in Step 2 as well. Again, a two-move setup for a double join. While it is fairly rare in this pure form, you can quite often get there with one or two setup moves. Since it's symmetric, you can do it in two ways.

Step 5+6 Full Index

This is a complete index of all possible corner positions in step 5, and some good ways to solve them. It does not deal with edge issues at all, so after this you are left with step 7.

The 7 possible "corner looks" are named A–G based on how the top-colour stickers of the corners form patterns. There are 27 different ways that the corners can be arranged (3×3×3 = 27, since corners 1, 2 & 3 can each show the top colour three ways, and the 4th is determined). For each of those, there are 6 different ways the corners can be placed, giving 162 configurations in theory. Due to symmetries, there are really just 32 distinct ones.

Of my two indexes, Step 5+6 is more important to know than Step 6+7. So start here. Apart from Niklas, Sune etc from the main tutorial, 8 sequences are named for use in this index. Most solutions are combinations of the ~15 named sequences. Clix (Cx), Evelyn (Ey), and Eve (EE) are probably the most important.

Step 6+7 Full Index

This is a complete index of all 50 possible positions in step 6, and how to best solve them. In step 6 all corners are in the right position but (probably) twisted wrong, and all edges twisted right but (probably) in the wrong position.

There are 27 possible corner configurations. For each there are 12 possible edge configurations. So in total 27×12 = 324 different positions. Fortunately many are really the same position — just mirrored or rotated — leaving only 50 distinct positions. One is the solved position, and 2 (C1 and D1) are really the same position.

For all positions, a simple solution is given that almost only consists of Sune, AntiSune, Rune, Bruno, and the step 7 sequences. Those use an average of 14.1 moves and at most 20. For about half the positions there are better known solutions ("perfect sequences") using an average of 12.9 moves. These were produced by Bernard Helmstetter using a computer program.

These are not speed solutions. Each took several minutes, thinking hard and studying the cube carefully for each turn. Physical — Speed & Dexterity

Though the mental side may be the most interesting, there is also a strong physical side to speed cubing. You need a good cube and fast fingers, to have any chance of achieving world class speed!

The cube itself

These days it's hard to find a bad cube. A cube you find at a regular retailer is most likely a good speed cube. Springs in the "skeleton" are vital to quality cubing. You can sense if there is one by pulling on a layer — it should have a "springy" give of 1 millimetre or so. Do NOT buy a springless cube!

Though some cubes can get pretty good without treatment, I recommend that you lube it. Many lubricants work. Silicone spray works for months and is what all speed cubers use. You can get it cheaply at any automotive store.

Dexterity — Think in "columns"

Every sequence of two (often more) moves can be done with column moves, that halves the number of movements you need to make. It takes a while to get to see sequences as moving columns instead of separate turns, but it's very easy once you "get" it.

The Trigger

Hold with your left hand the thumb on the near face and the middle finger on the far face. The index finger should not be part of the grip, but should press gently on the corner sticker during the first turn. When it is about ¾ complete, you can turn the top layer with the index finger. Meanwhile, start gently turning back. When the second turn is ¾ done, you will be able to do the third turn instantly.

This can be done ridiculously fast, 0.1 seconds or so. But start practising slower…

Sune™ (7 moves) at top speed takes 0.70 seconds — that's 7 moves in 30 frames, with more than one full turn done in a single frame.

Mental — The Zen of the Cube

Being able to solve Rubik's Cube very fast is a near useless skill, that takes a lot of time to acquire, and does not typically impress the opposite sex. If you think you have better things to do, I can only agree. You probably have.

Still, I think that learning to do something really well is important for anybody, and it's not too important what it is. If you have mastered one thing, it is a bit easier to master others. And doing something you're really good at is uplifting, relaxing and fun. Cubing is certainly cheaper than golf!

How to practise

Of course, you need to practise speed cubing a lot to get fast. But you also need to spend a lot of time thinking. Trying to solve in as few moves as possible, while thinking carefully about each turn. You will not learn how to do something fast until you learned how to do it slow!

Cube Nirvana

Strangely, for me, progress was a lot of quantum leaps. I would consistently solve the cube in about the same time, for example 80–90 seconds. Then suddenly one day I would achieve 67 seconds! And from then on I would perform in that range for some weeks, until I suddenly did 52 seconds. In this manner I progressed from 3 minutes down to about 25 seconds in a series of leaps.

Another weird thing is that I do not think at all when cubing. I can talk freely about anything while doing world-class speed. It seems like I just need to hook up my eyes and hands to some independent processor part of my brain, and it will just solve it before my eyes. It's a bit strange, but a pretty nice feeling!
