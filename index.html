Introduction & The Basic Idea

This is a tutorial for my method of solving Rubik's Cube. It is intended both for beginners and experienced cubers. There is no group theory or written notation involved, I just show you how to move.

Using this method, I won the 1981 Swedish championships, and finished 4th in the first world championships in Budapest 1982. I also won a minor world championship in 2005.

The Basic Idea

Most people solve the cube layer by layer. This is a simple way for the human mind to approach the problem, but it is useless for speed cubing. No matter how good you are, you will use more than 100 moves. Going for speed, I use 55–60 moves on average. Going for few moves, I average 40–45.

The basic problem with the layer method is big, and obvious once you realize it. When you have completed the first layer, you can do nothing without breaking it up. So you break it, do something useful, then restore it. Break it, do something, restore it. Again and again. In a good solution you do something useful all the time. The first layer is in the way of the solution, not a part of it!

STEP 1 Build the 2×2×2 Corner

The goal in this step is to build a 2×2×2 block. Or more precisely, to place one corner and its three neighbouring edges in their correct positions, solving 4 out of the 20 cube pieces.

Beginner

For the beginner it is often easiest to pick a corner that you will always start with. We will use the blue-yellow-orange corner. This means that the three edges are the blue-orange, the orange-yellow, and the blue-yellow.

What if there IS NO blue-yellow-orange corner? That's normal. There are many different colour schemes on cubes in the world. The method works for any set of colours, so try to focus on the method, not the particular colours used as examples.

There are many special cases, but this is the basic way I solve it:

  1. Pair up the corner with an edge.
  2. Pair up another edge with a centre.
  3. Join the pairs from 1 & 2 to make a 2×2×1 block.
  4. Join the remaining edge with the 2 remaining centres.
  5. Put it all together in the final move.

STEP 2 Expand to 2×2×3

In step 2 we expand what we have to a 2×2×3 block. That is, we add one corner and two edges to the solved block, going from 4 to 7 solved pieces of 20.

Beginner

Choose one of the three possible corners to expand to. This is quite similar to how you work in step 1. But be sure not to break up the 2×2×2 block! You need to be aware where it is at all times.

STEP 3 Twist the Edges

The basic idea of the method is to solve the entire cube from here by just turning the 2 free sides. But if you try to do that, you soon discover that some edges are always twisted the wrong way. We call those the "bad" edges. Step 3 fixes that.

Beginner

1. Identify the bad edges. There are 7 edges not part of the 2×2×3. Pick any one of them, place it between its two centre pieces using only the 2 free layers. If it's twisted right, it's good. Otherwise it's bad. Make a note of it, and check the next edge. When checking later edges, do not try to keep the previous edges in place — once you know if an edge is good or bad, you know.

2. Turn bad edges into good edges. There are always an even number of bad edges. You can make them good in pairs using the 3-move sequence below.

STEP 4 Finish 2 Layers

Back to adding solved pieces! In step 4 we go from 7 to 12 solved pieces out of 20. This is clearly the hardest step — though the other steps are really easy!

You have only two sides you can turn, and halfway through the step you can't even do many of those moves, since they would destroy what you already built. Keep a firm grip around the 2×2×3 with one hand at all times.

Step 4a

Focus on getting one corner and its two adjacent edges in position. The same techniques you use in steps 1 and 2 can be used, but since you can only turn two sides, more manoeuvring is needed.

Note that you now have two completed 2×2×3 blocks! Remember which of them you started with. If you accidentally start using the other, bad edges will reappear, and you have to go back to Step 3.

STEP 5 Position the Corners

Now we're at the final layer. Here we do not think as much — we recognise patterns and apply rules.

Common mistake: If the edges are not twisted right here, you have either not done step 3 correctly, or screwed up the edges by breaking the 2×2×3 in step 4.

In this step we will place the top corners correctly, nothing else. The corners normally look pretty unsolved after completing step 5 — you have to inspect them to know they're right.

To do this we need a tool that moves the top corners without twisting the top edges. This tool is Niklas™.

Niklas™
L U' R' U L' U' R U2
7-turn sequence. Swaps the two corners opposite the layer where the first move is made. (Turn 8 is just alignment.)

  • 4/6 of the time, two adjacent corners have switched — one Niklas™.

STEP 6 Twist the Corners

So far we have the top edges twisted right, and the top corners placed right. The next step is twisting the corners right. This can be done using only one simple move sequence — Sune™. It twists three of the four top corners. Step 6 can always be done with 2 Sunes or less.

The two animations show both "mirrored" versions of Sune™. Note the big X — that is the target sticker of the Sune™.

The target of a Sune™ is the corner sticker on the opposite side from the first turn, on the side of the corner moving up. Look for where the black X's are in relation to the first move.

The rules (assuming a red top layer)

Rule 0: With no correct corners, target one of the four red stickers. After the Sune™ there will be one correct corner. Use Rule 1.

Block Building Patterns

The core of this method is to build blocks. It is what steps 1, 2 and 4 are all about. With experience you will pick up some patterns you can immediately recognise, which is one of the keys to doing this fast.

"Simple join"

The one I use the most. It simply consists of finding a corner and edge that are one move away from forming a pair.

F
F2

"Double join"

Very basic and very useful. The most common second goal once you've formed a pair. Since we're building a 2×2×1 block, a centre needs to be part of it as well as the corner and 2 edges.

F
F2

"Swing"

Brilliant when you can get it — pretty often for Step 1, occasionally in Step 2. The first move is just a setup move for a double join in the second move.

R' F2

"Double swing"

A very good Step 1 start. A two-move setup for a double join. Since it's symmetric, you can do it in two ways.

Step 5+6 Final Layer Index

A complete index of all possible corner positions in step 5, and some good ways to solve them. It does not deal with edge issues at all, so after this you are left with step 7.

There are 27 different ways the corners can be arranged, and for each there are 6 different corner placements, giving 162 configurations in theory. Due to symmetries, there are really just 32 distinct ones. The 7 "corner looks" are named A–G. The sub-pages yA through yG cover every case.

Apart from Niklas, Sune etc from the main tutorial, 8 sequences are named for use in this index. Clix, Evelyn and Eve are probably the most important.

Step 6+7 Final Layer Index

A complete index of all 50 possible positions in step 6, and how to best solve them. In step 6 all corners are in the right position but (probably) twisted wrong, and all edges twisted right but (probably) in the wrong position.

For all positions a simple solution is given using mainly Sune, AntiSune, Rune and Bruno — averaging 14.1 moves, at most 20. About half the positions also have better "perfect sequences" averaging 12.9 moves, produced by Bernard Helmstetter.

The 7 corner groups A–G:

  • A — All corners correct. Step 7 only.
  • B — One correct corner. A single Sune can fix the corners.
  • C — Two adjacent correct corners, the others "sideways".
  • D — Two adjacent correct corners, the others "on top".
  • E — Two diagonal correct corners.
  • F — Four incorrect corners "on toe". Land of the Double Sune.
  • G — Four incorrect corners "on heels". Let Bruno guide you.
